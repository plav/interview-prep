#SHEET Data structures
Q Design an LRU cache with O(1) get and put.
A I need fast lookup by key and fast updates to recency order. A hash map gives O(1) lookup, and a doubly linked list gives O(1) moves and removals, so the map points to list nodes, the most recent at the head and the least recent at the tail. On get, I move the node to the head. On put, I insert at the head, and if we're over capacity, I remove the tail and its map entry. In JavaScript I can use a Map, because it keeps insertion order: deleting and re-inserting a key moves it to the end, and the first key is the least recently used.
F Why a doubly linked list? || Removing a node in O(1) needs its previous pointer.
F Hash map or balanced tree? || A hash map is O(1) on average and unordered; a balanced tree is O(log n) but keeps keys sorted, for range queries.
F When is a heap better than sorting? || When you only need the top k items, or items arrive continuously: O(n log k) instead of O(n log n).
W Picks a structure without saying why.
S Names the operations needed, and picks the structure that makes each one cheap.
W Gives time complexity only.
S Gives time and space, notes average vs worst case, and checks edge cases.
#SHEET Algorithms
Q How do you recognise a dynamic programming problem, and how do you approach it?
A The signals are 'count the ways', 'minimum or maximum', or 'is it possible', where choices at each step depend on earlier ones and the brute-force recursion repeats the same subproblems. I first write that brute-force recursion to find the state, say 'fewest coins for amount a', then the transition from smaller states and the base case. Then I either memoise the recursion or fill a table bottom-up, and finally check whether I only need the last row or two, to cut memory. I'd also test a greedy idea against a small counterexample, because that's usually why DP is needed.
F BFS or DFS? || BFS for shortest paths in unweighted graphs, since it explores level by level. DFS for exploring everything, cycles, components and backtracking.
F How do you detect a cycle in a directed graph? || Topological sort: if not every node gets processed, there's a cycle. Or DFS with a "currently on the path" marker.
F What's the complexity of Dijkstra? || O((V + E) log V) with a binary heap.
W Jumps straight to a memorised solution.
S Builds from brute force to the optimised version, naming the state and the transition.
W States the complexity without reasoning.
S Derives it from the loops, or the number of states times the work per state.
#SHEET LeetCode patterns
Q Find the length of the longest substring without repeating characters.
A Brute force checks every substring for repeats, which is O(n³), or O(n²) with a set. Since it's a contiguous substring with a condition, a sliding window fits. I keep a left and right pointer and a map of each character's last index. As the right pointer moves, if the character was last seen inside the window, I move the left pointer just past it. The window is always valid, so I track the maximum length. Each pointer only moves forward, so it's O(n) time and O(k) space, where k is the alphabet size. Edge cases: an empty string returns 0, and all-identical characters return 1.
F Why does this work? || The window always contains unique characters, and every valid window ending at each position is considered once.
F What if it's at most two distinct characters instead? || The same window, with a count map: shrink while the map holds more than two keys.
F How do you recognise sliding window problems? || Contiguous subarrays or substrings, with "longest", "shortest" or "count" under a condition.
W Starts coding immediately, in silence.
S Clarifies, gives brute force and its cost, names the pattern from the signals, then codes.
W "I think it works."
S Walks through an example, covers edge cases, and states time and space.
#SHEET Coding Interview University roadmap
Q How did you prepare for this interview?
A I refreshed fundamentals with a structured plan, using parts of Coding Interview University for data structures, graphs and dynamic programming, then focused on the roughly fifteen problem patterns that come up most, solving a few problems for each, out loud and timed. I also did weekly mock interviews, and prepared stories about real projects using the STAR format. #SHEET Capstone project plan
Q Walk me through a project you built.
A I built an order service for a small shop, end to end on AWS, to practise production engineering. It's a TypeScript API on ECS Fargate with PostgreSQL, built entirely with Terraform and deployed through GitHub Actions using OIDC. Orders publish events through a transactional outbox to EventBridge and SQS, so emails and other side effects are decoupled and idempotent. Payments use Stripe with idempotency keys and webhooks, and a daily reconciliation job. It's traced with OpenTelemetry into Datadog, with SLO-based alerts. The most interesting problem was duplicate emails during a game day, which led me to add an idempotent consumer, and I wrote up the postmortem. (Use once you've built it.)
F Why ECS rather than EKS? || Name your ADR's reasoning: less operational overhead for one service, and EKS was a stretch goal to compare.
F What would you change at ten times the traffic? || Talk about read replicas or caching, partitioning hot paths, queue-based load levelling, and load-test evidence.
F What went wrong? || Tell a real incident from your game days, with the timeline, the fix and what you changed.
W Lists technologies used.
S Explains the problem, key decisions and trade-offs, and one thing that broke and how you fixed it.
#SHEET Common interview questions
I Tell me about yourself. || What they're testing: communication, and relevance to the role. Use the one-minute arc from the Interview skills sheet: where you are now, what you've done that matters for this role, and why this role next.
I Tell me about a project you're proud of. || What they're testing: ownership, impact and technical depth. Use your technical achievement story, or the capstone.
I Tell me about a time you disagreed with someone. || What they're testing: collaboration, influence and maturity. Use your disagreement story: evidence, listening, and disagree and commit.
I Tell me about a mistake or failure. || What they're testing: honesty, learning and accountability. Use an incident or failure story, ending with what changed.
I Describe a time you worked under pressure. || What they're testing: prioritisation and calm. Use an incident story (see the Incident management sheet).
I How did you handle unclear requirements? || What they're testing: judgement and proactivity. Use an ambiguity story: clarifying questions and small experiments.
I Tell me about leading without authority. || What they're testing: influence and leadership. Use a story about driving a change, mentoring, or an RFC (see the Written design proposals sheet).
I Describe a time you improved a process. || What they're testing: initiative and measurable impact. Use an automation or process story (see the Automation sheet).
I How do you prioritise competing work? || What they're testing: judgement and communication. Weigh impact against effort, and make the trade-offs visible to stakeholders.
I Tell me about giving or receiving difficult feedback. || What they're testing: self-awareness and empathy. Make it specific, kind and actionable, and say what you changed.
I Walk me through the architecture of your current system. || Prepare one diagram you can draw in two minutes: clients, services, data stores, messaging, and the biggest trade-off.
I How would you design X? || Use the System design sheet's method.
I How do you make a service reliable? || Prepare with the Advanced cloud principles and Incident management sheets.
I How do you secure an API? || Prepare with the Authentication and authorisation, APIs, and Security overview sheets.
I A query or endpoint is slow: what do you do? || Prepare with the SQL and Performance and load testing sheets.
I How do you test your code? || Prepare with the Dev testing and Unit tests sheets.
I How do you deploy safely? || Prepare with the CI/CD and Kubernetes: deploying sheets.
I Explain the difference between X and Y (SQL and NoSQL, queue and topic, process and thread). || Use the comparison tables across the sheets.
I What's a technology you've learned recently, and how? || A real example, with how you applied it.
I How do you use AI tools in your work? || See AI-assisted development (Ways of Working doc) and the AI-Assisted Engineering doc: productivity, with review and verification.
I Why are you leaving? || Forward-looking: "I'm looking for more ownership of X and to work with Y". Nothing negative about your employer.
I What's your biggest weakness? || A real, non-critical one, plus what you're doing about it, with evidence of progress.
I Why is there a gap in your CV? || Brief and honest (caring, study, health, redundancy), then what you did, and why you're ready now.
I Why should we hire you? || Three points matching their stated needs, each backed by evidence.
I What are your salary expectations? || Research a range (levels data, recruiters); give a range based on the role's scope, or ask for their budget first; negotiate the whole package.
I Where do you see yourself in five years? || Growth aligned with what the role offers.
I Do you have any questions for us? || Always yes: two or three from the Interview skills sheet.
I Tell me about a time you failed to deliver. || The situation, your part honestly, the impact, and what you changed afterwards.
